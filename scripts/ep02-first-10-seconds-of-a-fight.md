# EP02 — What Happens to Your Body in the First 10 Seconds of a Fight
Target: ~7:00 | VO ~1,050 words @ 150–160 wpm | Style: D_3D_dark (engine/reference-ep00)

## // 00 — COLD OPEN (0:00–0:25)
CAMERA: black → hard cut to stickman mid-shot, a second figure shoves him. Freeze frame. Body turns X-ray, brain glows red. Timecode overlay `T+0.0s` starts ticking in the corner and stays for the whole video.
SFX: shove impact, one deep heartbeat, silence, ticking clock.
TEXT: `7 DECISIONS. 0 PERMISSION.`

VO:
Second zero. Someone shoves you.
Before you've even decided what to do, your body has already made seven decisions for you.
In the next ten seconds, it will shut off your hearing, shrink your vision, and switch off pain so well that some people don't notice they're hurt until it's over.
Let's slow it down. Second by second.
And stay until second eight — because the thing everyone says happens in a fight… isn't actually happening.

## // 01 — T+0.0s · THE ALARM (0:25–1:10)
CAMERA: dive through the eye along the optic nerve → into the head, amygdala lights red, a pulse shoots to the hypothalamus. Label cards: `AMYGDALA`, `HYPOTHALAMUS`. Visual cortex region stays grey/"loading".
SFX: whoosh into skull, electric zap, alarm ping.
TEXT: `YOU'RE A PASSENGER.`

VO:
Here's the strange part. The fight starts before you see it.
Light hits your eyes, and that signal races to the amygdala — an almond-sized alarm deep in your brain.
The amygdala doesn't wait for the full picture. It fires a distress signal to the hypothalamus, your command center.
And the wiring is so fast that this whole cascade starts before the visual part of your brain has finished processing what you're looking at.
That's why people jump out of the way of a car before they know there's a car.
Your body is reacting to a threat your conscious mind hasn't seen yet.
You're not driving right now. You're a passenger.

## // 02 — T+1s · ADRENALINE (1:10–2:05)
CAMERA: signal runs down the spine (red line) → two small glands on top of the kidneys light up → red particles flood the blood vessels. Heart in close-up, data card `HR 72 → 150+ BPM` counting up.
SFX: heartbeat speeds up under this whole section.
INTERACTIVE: on-screen hand with two fingers on the neck, text `CHECK YOUR PULSE`.

VO:
The hypothalamus fires down your nerves to two small glands sitting on top of your kidneys — the adrenal glands.
And they dump adrenaline straight into your blood.
Try this. Put two fingers on the side of your neck. Feel that?
That's your resting pulse. For most people, somewhere between sixty and a hundred beats a minute.
In a real fight, adrenaline can push that well past double, in seconds.
Every beat gets harder, not just faster.
Your airways open wider to pull in more oxygen.
Your pupils open wider to let in more light.
Your liver starts dumping stored sugar into your blood. Fuel.
Your body is spending everything it has on one thing: the next ten seconds.

## // 03 — T+2–3s · THE SHUTDOWN (2:05–2:55)
CAMERA: fast tour of the X-ray body: stomach dims to grey (`DIGESTION: OFF`), mouth/glands dim (`SALIVA: ↓`), skin and hands fade pale, legs/chest/shoulders glow brighter. Hands in close-up start trembling.
SFX: power-down clicks for each organ, low hum for the muscles.
TEXT: `PRECISION → POWER`

VO:
To pay for that, it shuts down everything it doesn't need.
Digestion? Stops. That's the knot in your stomach.
Saliva drops. That's the dry mouth.
Blood vessels in your skin clamp down, and blood gets pushed toward the big muscles — legs, chest, shoulders. That's why faces go pale.
And it's why your hands start shaking.
Big movements get stronger. Small movements — getting a key in a door, typing a phone number — get worse.
Your body just traded precision for power.
I'm studying to be a physiotherapist, and this is the part I find wild: in about two seconds, your muscles get rewired for one single job.

## // 04 — T+4s · TUNNEL VISION (2:55–3:45)
CAMERA: POV shot. The frame edges blur and go dark, a circle closes in on the opponent's hands. Then pull out to show a second stickman standing in the dark zone.
SFX: the room ambience narrows, a single high tone.
INTERACTIVE: `STARE AT THE CENTER` with a red dot in the middle of the screen.
DATA CARD: `79% — TUNNEL VISION` (police survey, Artwohl 2002)

VO:
Quick test. Stare at the red dot in the middle of your screen.
Without moving your eyes — how much of the room can you still see? The walls. The edge of your desk. Maybe your phone.
Now imagine most of that just… disappearing.
When a police psychologist surveyed 157 officers who'd been in shootings, 79% described tunnel vision.
The brain locks onto the threat — the hands, the face — and everything at the edges stops existing.
Which sounds useful.
Until you realize the second guy is standing in the part you can't see.

## // 05 — T+5s · THE SILENCE (3:45–4:25)
CAMERA: dive into the ear, cochlea glows then fades out. Waveform on screen flattens to a line.
SFX: crowd and shouting → muffled, like underwater → near silence, only the heartbeat.
DATA CARD: `84% — SOUND DIMINISHED`

VO:
Then the sound goes.
In that same survey, 84% of officers said sounds were diminished.
Gunshots sounded like pops. People shouting right next to them — barely there.
It's called auditory exclusion.
Your brain can't process everything at once, so it cuts the channel it thinks you need least.
In a fight, that's usually the person yelling "stop."

## // 06 — T+6–7s · PAIN: OFF (4:25–5:15)
CAMERA: impact on the stickman's hand (red flash at the knuckles) → pain signal travels up the arm → a blue "gate" in the spinal cord/brain closes and the red signal fades. Molecule icons `ENDORPHINS` flood the brain.
SFX: crack, then a soft mute "thump", heartbeat continues.
TEXT: `NOT PROTECTING YOU. HIDING THE DAMAGE.`

VO:
And then the strangest switch of all. Pain.
Under acute stress, your brain releases its own opioids — endorphins, the same family of chemicals as morphine.
Scientists call it stress-induced analgesia.
In one experiment, first-time parachutists became less sensitive to pain just from the fear of the jump.
When they were given a drug that blocks opioids, the effect disappeared.
That's why fighters finish rounds with broken hands.
Why people walk away from car crashes and only notice the injury when they sit down.
Your body isn't protecting you from damage.
It's hiding the damage — so you keep going.

## // 07 — T+8s · THE TIME ILLUSION (5:15–6:10)
CAMERA: everything goes into slow motion (punch frozen in mid-air) → hard cut to a stickman falling backwards into a net from a tall tower, wrist screen flashing numbers. Data cards: `31 M FREE FALL`, `+36% FELT LONGER`, `NUMBERS READ: 0`. Then into the brain: amygdala "records" extra frames like a film strip.
SFX: time-stretch whoosh, wind rush during the fall, record-scratch at the reveal.
TEXT: `TIME DIDN'T SLOW DOWN.`

VO:
Now — the thing I promised.
Almost everyone who's been in a fight or a crash says the same thing: time slowed down.
In that police survey, 62% said it felt like slow motion.
So in 2007, neuroscientist David Eagleman tested it.
He dropped volunteers thirty-one meters — about ten floors — in free fall, into a net.
On their wrist: a screen flashing numbers too fast for a normal brain to read.
If time really slowed down, they'd be able to read them.
Afterwards, they said their own fall lasted 36% longer than other people's.
But they couldn't read the numbers. Not better than normal.
Time didn't slow down.
Your amygdala just records the moment in so much detail that when you remember it, it feels longer.
That's also why ten seconds of a fight can replay in your head for years.
The slow motion isn't happening during the fight. It's happening after — in your memory.

## // 08 — T+9–10s · THE CRASH (6:10–7:05)
CAMERA: film strip in the brain with missing frames (black gaps). Then the fight ends: stickman stands alone, red particles still in the blood but no target. Legs and hands vibrate. Blue wave (`PARASYMPATHETIC: ON`) washes over the body, colors go from red back to blue.
SFX: heartbeat slowing down, shaky breathing, low drone fading.
DATA CARD: `52% — COULDN'T REMEMBER PARTS OF IT`
INTERACTIVE (end of section): lungs in X-ray, `INHALE · INHALE · EXHALE` with a breathing ring; heart-rate card counts down.

VO:
Which is also why memories of fights are so unreliable.
In the same survey, more than half the officers couldn't remember parts of what happened.
Some didn't remember firing their weapon at all.
Then, when it ends — the bill arrives.
The adrenaline is still in your blood, but there's nothing left to spend it on.
Your legs shake. Your hands shake. Some people feel sick. Some laugh. Some cry.
That's not weakness.
That's a system built for a predator attack, trying to switch off in a parking lot.
And finally, your brake — the parasympathetic nervous system — kicks in.
And here's the one lever you actually control.
Your breath.
Two short inhales through the nose, then one long, slow exhale through the mouth.
In a 2023 Stanford study, five minutes of this a day improved mood more than mindfulness meditation did.
The long exhale is the fastest way to tell your body: it's over.
Try it right now. Inhale… inhale again… and let it all out.
