## // 01 — SECOND ZERO · THE ALARM (0:38–1:05)

ON SCREEN: SECOND 0 · ALARM. · YOU'RE NOT DRIVING.

VO:
Second zero. Someone shoves you.
Your eyes catch it — and a tiny alarm deep in your brain goes off. It's called the amygdala.
It doesn't wait to understand what's happening. It just hits the panic button.
That's why you can jump away from a car before you even know there's a car.
Right now, you're not driving. You're the passenger.

Second one. Adrenaline.
Try this. Put two fingers on the side of your neck. Feel that? That's your resting pulse — for most people, sixty to a hundred beats a minute.

Your lungs open up to pull in more air.
Your pupils get bigger to let in more light.
Your liver dumps sugar into your blood. Fuel.
Your whole body is going all-in on the next ten seconds.

## // 03 — SECONDS TWO–THREE · THE TRADE (1:50–2:35)
PICTURE: fast cuts — stomach knot (grey), dry mouth, pale face, trembling hands trying to put a key in a door / type a phone number; legs and shoulders flex.
ON SCREEN: SECOND 2 · STOMACH: OFF. · SALIVA: OFF. · PALE. · SHAKING. · PRECISION → POWER

VO:
To pay for that, your body shuts off everything it doesn't need.
Digestion stops. That's the knot in your stomach.
Saliva drops. That's the dry mouth.
Blood leaves your skin and rushes to your big muscles. That's why faces go pale.
And it's why your hands shake.
Big moves get stronger. Small moves — a key in a lock, typing a number — get worse.
Your body just traded precision for power.
And here's the wild part: in about two seconds, your muscles get rewired for one job.

## // 04 — SECOND FOUR · TUNNEL VISION (2:35–3:15)
PICTURE: POV of B. Red dot in the center of the screen, edges go dark and blurry. Pull out: a second guy is standing in the dark part.
ON SCREEN: SECOND 4 · STARE AT THE DOT · TUNNEL VISION. · 79% OF OFFICERS

VO:
Second four. Quick test. Stare at the red dot in the middle of your screen.
Without moving your eyes — how much of the room can you still see?
Now imagine all of that just… disappearing.
When a police psychologist asked 157 officers who'd been in shootings, 79% said they got tunnel vision.
Your brain locks onto the threat. Everything else stops existing.
Sounds useful. Until the second guy is standing in the part you can't see.

In a fight, that's usually the person yelling "stop."

## // 07 — SECOND EIGHT · THE LIE (4:25–5:20)
PICTURE: the punch freezes in slow motion → hard cut: stickman falls backwards from a tall tower into a net, a screen on his wrist flashes numbers. Cards: 31 M · +36% · 0 NUMBERS. Then a film strip with extra frames.
ON SCREEN: SECOND 8 · THE LIE. · SLOW MOTION? · TIME DIDN'T SLOW DOWN.

VO:
Second eight. The lie I promised.

In that police survey, 62% said it felt like slow motion.
So a neuroscientist named David Eagleman tested it.
He dropped people thirty-one meters — about ten floors — into a net.
On their wrist: a screen flashing numbers too fast to read.

Afterwards, they said their fall felt 36% longer.
But they couldn't read the numbers.
Time didn't slow down.
Your brain just records the moment in so much detail that, when you remember it, it feels longer.
The slow motion doesn't happen during the fight. It happens after — in your memory.

## // 09 — HOW TO STAY IN CONTROL (5:55–6:55)
PICTURE: replay of the standoff from the hook — but this time B is calm: breathing ring around his chest, then his "camera" widens and sees the second guy and the exit. Three numbered steps on screen.
ON SCREEN: STAY IN CONTROL · 1. NAME IT. "I'M READY." · 2. BREATHE. IN–IN–OUT · 3. WIDEN. · THE EDGE: A CLEAR HEAD

VO:
So here's how to stay in control — what I promised.
One. Name it. When your heart slams, don't fight it. Tell yourself: "This is my body getting ready."
In a Harvard study, people who said "I'm excited" before a stressful task did better than people who told themselves to calm down.

Two. Breathe. Two short breaths in through the nose… and one long breath out.
The long exhale is your body's brake pedal.

Three. Widen. Tunnel vision wants you to stare. Look around on purpose — who else is there, where's the exit.
That's the edge most people never get. Not a harder punch. A clear head.
And a clear head usually makes the smartest move of all: walking away before it starts.

## FACTS & SOURCES
1. Amygdala → hypothalamus → adrenal glands; the cascade starts before the visual centres finish processing (jumping out of a car's path). — Harvard Health, "Understanding the stress response": https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/understanding-the-stress-response
2. Adrenaline: faster/stronger heartbeat, airways open, glucose released; resting HR 60–100 bpm (AHA / Mayo Clinic). "Can more than double" is hedged ("can").
3. Officer survey, n=157: 79% tunnel vision, 84% diminished sound, 62% slow motion, 52% memory loss for part of the event. — Artwohl (2002), FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin 71(10):18–24.
4. Stress-induced analgesia via endogenous opioids (blocked by naloxone). — Bandura et al. 1988 (PubMed 3171918).
5. 31 m free fall into a net; own fall rated 36% longer; could not read the fast numbers → memory effect. — Stetson, Fiesta, Eagleman (2007), PLoS ONE 2(12):e1295.
6. "I am excited" vs "I am calm" (singing, public speaking, math): excited reappraisal improved performance (e.g. karaoke 80% vs 69%, math +8%). — Brooks (2014), J Exp Psychol Gen 143(3):1144–58 (Harvard Business School). https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2013/12/performance-anxiety
7. Police RCT: officers trained to manage physiological stress (incl. breathing control) outperformed controls on physiological control, situational awareness and correct use-of-force decisions (p < .01). — Andersen & Gustafsberg (2016), "A Training Method to Improve Police Use of Force Decision Making", SAGE Open. https://doaj.org/article/240e264d93624ffe81321e6cd0a0af64
8. Cyclic sighing (double inhale, long exhale) lowers physiological arousal. — Balban et al. (2023), Cell Reports Medicine 4(1):100895.
