# EP01 — Your Brain Is Lying to You Right Now (I Can Prove It)

- Length: ~7:00 · Style: 3D dark (see STYLE_GUIDE.md) · Voice: creator's own
- Format: 7 "lies" your brain tells you, weakest → strongest. The viewer tests each one on themselves.
- Retention device: a word list shown at 0:30 that the viewer must remember. The payoff (false memory) is the last lie.
- Status: **script draft v1** — waiting for creator edits and the voice recording.

Legend: `VO` = voiceover (English, read exactly). `CAM` = camera/visual. `SFX` = sound. **bold** = stress the word. `…` = ~1 s pause.

---

## 0:00 — HOOK
CAM: Dark studio. A 3D stickman sits facing a glowing screen (he is "the viewer"). Camera pushes into his eye. On every "blind" → hard black frame.
SFX: low drone; on each "blind" a click + 1 frame of total silence.

VO:
> Right now… your brain is lying to you.
> Not *sometimes*. **Right now.** While you're watching **this**.
> Every time your eyes move — and they move up to three times every second — you go **blind**. Completely.
> Your brain cuts the footage… and fills the gap so smoothly that you've never noticed. Not once. In your entire life.
> And that's not even the **strangest** thing your brain **hides** from you.
> Stay with me — because in the next **few** minutes, I'm going to **prove** it. On you.

## 0:30 — OPEN LOOP: memory test
VO:
> But first — a quick test. I'm going to show you fifteen words. Don't write them down. Just read… and remember as many as you can. I'll come back to them at the very end.

CAM: Big 3D words, one by one, ~0.7 s each, floating in the dark:
`bed · rest · awake · tired · dream · wake · snooze · blanket · doze · slumber · snore · nap · peace · yawn · drowsy`
**Do NOT show the word "sleep". It is the trap.**
SFX: soft tick per word.

VO:
> Got them? Good. Hold on to them.

## 1:00 — LIE #7: You can see your nose
CAM: POV from inside the stickman's head; the edge of his nose glows with a red outline. Then pull out to show the brain "filtering" — constant signals greyed out (nose, clothes, a humming fridge icon).

VO:
> Lie number seven. You can see your nose. Right now. All the time.
> Close one eye… there it is.
> It's been in your view every second of your life — but your brain decided it's useless… and erased it.
> Your brain filters out anything that never changes. Your nose. The feeling of your clothes. The hum of your fridge.
> You don't experience the world. You experience the parts your brain thinks matter.

## 1:45 — LIE #6: There's a hole in your vision (TEST)
CAM: Fly into a 3D eye to the back wall (retina). Show where the optic nerve exits — highlight it as an empty zone with a mono label `NO PHOTORECEPTORS`.

VO:
> Lie number six. There's a hole in your vision. Literally.
> In the back of each eye, there's a spot where the optic nerve leaves — and there are no light receptors there. Zero.
> So why don't you see a black hole floating in front of you? Let's find it.

CAM: Full-screen TEST card, hold 8–10 s: white **+** on the left, red **●** on the right, ~15 cm apart on a laptop. Small note: `works best on a computer or tablet`.

VO:
> Close your left eye. Stare at the cross with your right eye… and slowly move closer to your screen.
> At some point — the dot **disappears**.
> That's your blind spot. And your brain just paints over it with whatever's around. It's been inventing that part of your world your entire life.

## 2:40 — LIE #5: You can't tickle yourself
CAM: Stickman tries to tickle himself. X-ray of the head: the cerebellum lights up and sends a "prediction" pulse; a signal meter drops to low. Second stickman tickles him → meter maxes, he squirms.

VO:
> Lie number five. Try to tickle yourself. Go ahead.
> …Nothing.
> Because a part of your brain — the cerebellum — predicts every move you make, before you make it. It knows exactly what your fingers will feel… so it turns the signal down.
> Someone else's hand? No prediction. Full signal.
> Your brain isn't just receiving the world. It's constantly **guessing** it.

## 3:20 — LIE #4: The frozen clock (TEST)
CAM: Huge 3D analog clock with a red second hand. Camera looks away, then snaps to the clock; the first tick visibly "stretches".

VO:
> Lie number four. Ever looked at a clock… and the second hand seemed to freeze — longer than a second?
> You're not imagining it. Look away from this clock… now look right at it.
> When your eyes jump to the clock, your brain fills that blind moment with what it sees **after** — so the first second feels longer than it really was.
> Scientists call it chronostasis. Your brain literally bends time to hide a gap.

## 4:05 — LIE #3: You go blind (callback to the hook)
CAM: Stickman at a mirror. His eyes jump left/right; on every jump the frame goes black for a beat. Counter in the corner adds up the "blind time".

VO:
> Lie number three — the one from the beginning.
> Next time you're at a mirror, try this. Look at your left eye. Now your right. Left. Right.
> You will never see your eyes move. **Never.** Someone next to you sees them move every time.
> But you can't — because during every jump, your brain switches your vision off.
> Up to three times a second. All day long. By some estimates — that adds up to around **forty minutes a day**. Forty minutes… your brain quietly deletes.

## 4:55 — LIE #2: You live in the past
CAM: Three signal streams (eyes = light, ears = sound waves, skin = pulse) race into the brain at different speeds. The brain "waits", stacks them, stitches one frame. Timer in the corner: `−0.08 s`.

VO:
> Lie number two. You think you live in the present. You don't.
> Signals from your eyes, ears and skin arrive at different speeds. So your brain waits — collects everything — and stitches it into one clean moment.
> Research suggests that wait is about **eighty milliseconds**.
> So everything you see right now… has already happened. You're always living a little bit in the past. Your brain just never tells you.

## 5:35 — LIE #1: Your memory lies (payoff of the test)
CAM: The 3D words return and swirl. Red countdown on each question.

VO:
> And lie number one. The biggest.
> Remember those words from the beginning? Don't scroll back.
> Was **"bed"** on the list? …Yes.
> Was **"dream"**? …Yes.
> And was the word **"sleep"** on the list?
> …Think carefully.

SFX: 2 s of total silence. Then the biggest impact in the video.

VO:
> **"Sleep" was never on the list.** Not once.
> If you were sure you saw it — you're not alone. In the original experiment, people "remembered" words like this almost half the time.
> Your brain didn't store the list. It stored the **idea**… and then built a memory to fit it.
> And it does this with everything. Every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it. Your first day at school. Your last argument. The way someone looked at you.
> Some of it… never happened.

CAM: Memories as floating 3D frames; some of them crack and rebuild differently.

## 6:35 — OUTRO
CAM: Pull back from a translucent 3D head; the brain pulses in the dark.

VO:
> So — if your brain can delete forty minutes of your day, paint over a hole in your vision, and invent a memory in thirty seconds…
> how much of what you're sure about… is actually true?
> Tell me in the comments — did you remember **"sleep"**?
> And if you want to see what your body does when **you** lie… that's the next video.

End screen → next video: "Your Body Can't Lie".

## Fact notes (keep wording careful)
- Saccades: eyes make roughly 2–3 saccades per second; vision is suppressed during each one (saccadic masking). The "~40 minutes a day" figure is a popular estimate, not a precise measurement → always say "by some estimates".
- Blind spot: optic disc has no photoreceptors; the brain fills it in.
- Tickling: the cerebellum predicts the sensory result of your own movement and dampens it (Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith).
- Chronostasis: Yarrow et al., Nature 2001 — the first moment after a saccade is perceived as longer.
- Perception delay: Eagleman & Sejnowski (Salk, 2000) — perception integrates ~80 ms after an event.
- False memory: Roediger & McDermott 1996 (DRM). The non-presented word was falsely recalled on 40–55% of lists and falsely recognised ~72–84% of the time. Sleep word list taken from that paradigm.
